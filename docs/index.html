Escalation hides in relative amounts

We explored 10 million transactions behind 20,000 monitoring alerts. The clearest escalation signal is a customer whose cash moves at high amounts compared with their own bank transfers. Raw amounts hide it, because every customer has a different scale.

§1 · Approach

Turn each alert's history into a profile, then rank

Each alert owns hundreds of transaction rows, but a model needs one row per alert. Most of the work is deciding how to summarize a customer's history so that escalated and dismissed alerts separate.

We explored first, built features for every pattern the exploration suggested, and kept only what improved cross-validated AUC. We built two models on different feature sets; both reached the same conclusion, and the submission averages them.

Quality checks

  • No missing values or duplicates; every alert has transactions.
  • Train and test alert IDs don't overlap.
  • Every alert has 180 days of history up to its alert date and nothing from later days. A few rows (0.06%) are stamped on the alert date itself; they are equally common for escalated and dismissed alerts (17.4% vs 17.1% escalation), so they carry no signal. Model A keeps them and Model B drops them.
  • Train and test look alike: a classifier trying to tell them apart scores 0.49 AUC (chance).
  • No shortcuts: IDs, dates, timestamps and shared transactions carry no signal (≈ 0.50 AUC).

§4 · Transaction history

What 180 days of activity look like

Every history combines a slowly fading stream of everyday activity with a pile-up of transactions in the last minutes before midnight on the day before the alert.

§5 · Escalated vs dismissed

Simple behaviours barely differ. Relative amounts do.

We compared escalated and dismissed alerts on every obvious behaviour first. None of them separates the classes well, so we looked inside each customer's own profile.

Escalated customers move cash in at high amounts relative to their own bank transfers. Measured within the customer, this contrast is the strongest single predictor in the data.

cash-in vs bank-out = mean(miqdor | kirim, naqd) − mean(miqdor | chiqim, bank_otkazmasi) cash vs bank score = [mean(naqd) − mean(karta)] − [mean(bank_otkazmasi) − mean(karta)]

§7 · Model and validation

Two complementary models, one ensemble

Model A (1,084 features)

LightGBM, CatBoost and XGBoost with shallow trees (7 leaves, at least 100 alerts per leaf, strong regularization). 5-fold stratified CV repeated over 5 seeds with early stopping; 25 fold models per algorithm. AUC 0.655.

Model B (201 features)

LightGBM, 7 leaves × 300 trees, on direction × type statistics plus within-customer normalization, averaged over 5 seeds. AUC 0.653. The two agree on about 91% of their ranking, so averaging reduces each one's errors.

Validation

  • Out-of-fold scores only: every prediction comes from a model that never saw that alert.
  • Stable: model seeds move the score by ±0.0006, and folds agree within the expected noise.
  • No train/test shift (adversarial AUC 0.49), no transactions from after the alert date, and no identifiers used as features. Model A also includes the alert's month and weekday, which carry no signal on their own (AUC 0.51).
  • Test predictions average 17.3%, matching the training escalation rate of 17.2%.
  • Reproducible: one Jupyter notebook rebuilds the submitted file from the five original competition files (≈ 25 minutes, fixed seeds).
§8 · What it means for the monitoring unit

Review the riskiest alerts first

An AUC is abstract. In practice the model ranks the day's alerts so that specialists see the most likely escalations first. Drag the slider to choose how much of the queue to review.

§9 · How far can it go?

§10 · Conclusion

What we learned

  • The data is clean. Each alert has 180 days of prior history and nothing from later days, and train and test match, so cross-validation is a trustworthy guide.
  • Obvious triggers don't separate the classes. Volume, cash and international share, time of day and the final-hour pile-up look the same for escalated and dismissed alerts.
  • Relative amounts carry the signal. Cash inflows that are large compared with the same customer's bank transfers are the strongest escalation marker. Removing each customer's scale is what makes it visible.
  • Two different models agree. Built on different feature sets, both reached about 0.655 and rely on the same key features. Averaged, they reach , and the riskiest 10% of alerts escalate 3.5× as often as the safest 10%.
  • The ceiling is in the data. We found no leak, template or planted pattern, and accuracy doesn't improve with longer histories. Gains came from better features, not bigger models.

In one sentence: escalation is predicted less by how much money moves than by how a customer's cash compares with their own bank transfers.
